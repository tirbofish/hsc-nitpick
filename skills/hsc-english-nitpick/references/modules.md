# HSC English module lenses

Read only the section matching the student's course and module. These are audit lenses, not thesis templates. The exact question and its marking guidelines outrank this summary.

## Routing

| Course | Common Module | Module A | Module B | Module C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English Standard | Texts and human experiences | Language, identity and culture | Close study of literature | The craft of writing |
| English Advanced | Texts and human experiences | Textual conversations | Critical study of literature | The craft of writing |

The Common Module and Module C are shared in name, but judge the response against the exact course, task and marking criteria supplied. Do not assume that inserting syllabus terminology earns marks.

## Common Module — Texts and human experiences

### Core focus

Students examine how texts represent individual and collective human experiences, including the emotions and human qualities associated with them. Texts can reveal tensions, anomalies, paradoxes and inconsistencies in behaviour and motivation, and storytelling can shape how responders understand themselves and others.

### Audit for

- a precise answer about the human experience named in the question, not a catalogue of module concepts;
- distinction between an event in the plot and the composer's **representation** of that experience;
- a defensible relationship among experience, emotion, behaviour, motivation and consequence where relevant;
- analysis of how form, genre, structure and language shape the responder's understanding;
- coverage of every contrast or qualifier in the question, such as individual/collective, challenge/affirmation, paradox, change or insight;
- a personal interpretation when the question asks how the text enriches, challenges or changes understanding;
- evidence drawn from enough of the prescribed text to support a holistic argument.

### Common failures

- retelling hardship and calling it a human experience;
- naming an anomaly or paradox without explaining what expectations it disrupts;
- repeating “human experience” after evidence without identifying the insight produced;
- treating a character's experience as the composer's viewpoint without analysing construction;
- forcing every paragraph through the same memorised list of rubric terms.

## English Standard Module A — Language, identity and culture

### Core focus

Students examine how language, form and textual features represent and shape individual and collective identity, cultural experiences, values and belonging. Language may affirm, preserve, negotiate, challenge or exclude identities and cultural perspectives.

### Audit for

- clear identification of the person, group, culture or community under discussion;
- a causal chain from language choice or form to representation, then to an effect on identity, culture or belonging;
- attention to both parts when a question pairs benefits/challenges, individual/collective, language/culture or belonging/exclusion;
- specific cultural values, assumptions, tensions or practices rather than “culture” as an empty abstraction;
- awareness that identities and cultures can be dynamic, contested and internally diverse;
- accurate, relevant evidence and explanation of how it shapes meaning.

### Common failures

- describing characters' identities without analysing language;
- treating culture as nationality alone or making unsupported generalisations about a group;
- listing slang, code-switching, imagery or voice without connecting the feature to identity or culture;
- discussing belonging while ignoring the cultural or linguistic mechanism named in the question.

## English Standard Module B — Close study of literature

### Core focus

Students develop a detailed understanding of one prescribed text through close analysis of its ideas, values, attitudes and distinctive qualities. Form, language, structure, purpose, audience and context matter insofar as they shape meaning and the response to the text.

### Audit for

- close knowledge of the text as a whole, not one scene, character or theme recycled repeatedly;
- analysis of features distinctive to the text's form: dramatic action, poetic structure, narration, film language, documentary construction or other relevant conventions;
- a clear relationship among composer, purpose, form and the question;
- interpretation of specific values and attitudes rather than generic moral lessons;
- well-selected evidence that develops a sustained answer;
- integration of a supplied extract or image with the wider text when the task requires it.

### Common failures

- plot summary dressed as analysis;
- technique labels that could apply to any text;
- context added as biography without changing the interpretation;
- isolated quotations used to claim a “holistic” understanding;
- importing Advanced Module B terms such as textual integrity without making them relevant to the Standard question.

## English Advanced Module A — Textual conversations

### Core focus

Students evaluate the conversation between paired texts: how the later and earlier works echo, reframe, challenge, preserve or collide with one another across contexts. Changes in form, language, perspective, values and assumptions create new meanings and can alter appreciation of both texts.

### Audit for

- a thesis about the **relationship** between the texts, not two separate theme statements;
- balanced, integrated treatment of both texts throughout the argument;
- the direction and purpose of each connection: what is retained, transformed, resisted or newly exposed;
- comparison of form and textual features, not content alone;
- context used to explain changed meaning, values or reception rather than merely date each text;
- an evaluative judgement about why the conversation matters, especially when the question asks about value, appreciation, artistry or significance;
- recognition of complexity: homage can coexist with critique, and resonance with dissonance.

### Common failures

- “same theme, different context” with no account of the transformation;
- discussing the earlier text and later text in sealed halves;
- assuming the later text automatically corrects or improves the earlier one;
- using `reimagines`, `reframes` or `reshapes` without identifying the original perspective and the new one;
- uneven evidence or detailed analysis of only one text;
- treating contextual change as the argument instead of the cause of a specific change in meaning.

## English Advanced Module B — Critical study of literature

### Core focus

Students form a sustained personal and critical evaluation of a prescribed text through close study. They consider how ideas, values, context, form, language and structure contribute to the text's textual integrity, literary value and continuing capacity to invite interpretation.

### Audit for

- a conceptual thesis that makes an informed judgement about the exact question;
- close analysis of form-specific artistry and its relationship to ideas and values;
- a personal critical voice supported by evidence, not unsupported statements of enjoyment;
- context that sharpens evaluation of the text's construction, reception or value;
- coverage of the whole work, or the prescribed suite, with meaningful links among parts;
- integration of any supplied extract, scene, stanza or image into an interpretation of the whole text;
- accurate use of **textual integrity** to mean the coherence and unity of the text's elements, not moral correctness or factual accuracy;
- explanation of literary value rather than simply declaring the text timeless or universal.

### Common failures

- reproducing a prepared “textual integrity” essay around the edges of the question;
- treating each poem in a suite as an unrelated mini-essay;
- praising “artistry” without showing how a formal choice produces meaning;
- biographical or historical context that does not affect interpretation;
- a character or theme essay that ignores the text's construction and value.

## Module C — The craft of writing

### Core focus

Students compose purposeful imaginative, discursive, persuasive and/or reflective writing. They use language, structure, voice and form deliberately for audience, purpose and context, drawing on studied texts as models of craft rather than material to copy.

### Audit for the crafted piece

- exact compliance with the required form, perspective, audience and purpose;
- meaningful use of every compulsory stimulus or phrase, structurally or conceptually rather than as a detachable mention;
- a sustained and credible voice, controlled tone and deliberate structure;
- language choices that perform a purpose rather than accumulate decorative techniques;
- development proportionate to the mark value and time conditions;
- originality of treatment without sacrificing coherence, clarity or control.

### Audit for a reflection

- a clear statement of intent tied to the question;
- a precise chain from the student's own quoted choice to technique or structural decision, intended effect and meaning;
- explanation of how studied craft influenced the student's decision without claiming imitation or borrowing content;
- concise evaluation rather than a second creative piece or a list of techniques.

### Common failures

- a memorised piece with the stimulus attached at the beginning or end;
- violating the required form or changing voice without purpose;
- imagery, fragmented sentences or symbolism used decoratively and then overexplained;
- a reflection that says a device “engages the reader” without naming the intended response;
- analysing the prescribed text more than the student's own choices;
- claiming an effect the writing does not actually create.
